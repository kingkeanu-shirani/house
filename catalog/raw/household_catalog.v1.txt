“Do it without bothering the people in the room.” Requires social navigation.
“Clean while people are still using the kitchen.” Requires dynamically working around moving humans and active tasks.
“Work around me.” Requires continuous path and manipulation replanning.
“Wait until I’m done with that.” Requires recognizing task dependencies.
“Finish this after they leave.” Requires postponed task memory.
“Do this whenever you get a chance.” Requires scheduling around other priorities.
“Do this before it becomes a problem.” Requires predictive maintenance reasoning.
“Only clean things that actually need it.” Requires determining condition rather than following a fixed schedule.
“Don’t throw away anything important.” Requires high precision and conservative uncertainty handling.
“Throw away anything obviously useless.” Requires semantic and personal-value judgment.
“Get rid of the junk.” Very difficult because one person's junk may be another person's valuable possession.
“Sort this pile into what we keep, donate, and throw away.” Requires household preferences and sometimes asking for confirmation.
“Organize this however makes the most sense.” Requires generating its own organizational system.
“Make this easier for me to find later.” Requires modeling human retrieval behavior.
“Put the things we use most somewhere convenient.” Requires frequency-of-use memory.
“Move rarely used stuff out of the way.” Requires long-term usage statistics.
“Reorganize this room now that we bought this new thing.” Requires adapting an existing organization system.
“Figure out what changed and update everything accordingly.” A general reconfiguration problem.
“Do the rest the same way I did this one.” Requires imitation from a single example.
“Watch me do this once, then do the others.” Few-shot physical learning.
“Do this like I showed you last time.” Long-term skill memory.
“Copy what that person is doing.” Requires action interpretation and embodiment transfer.
“Do something similar, but for this object.” Requires abstraction rather than imitation.
“Use your best judgment.” The robot has to choose without explicit procedural instruction.
“Figure it out.” Possibly the ultimate generalization benchmark: inspect state, infer intent, construct a plan, act, detect failure, recover, and know when to ask for help.
Physics and Reflexive Edge Cases
“Catch this falling object.” Requires dynamic reflexive control, predicting the trajectory of an object in freefall, and deciding whether it is safe to catch (e.g., catching a falling cup vs. letting a falling knife hit the floor).
“Open this carbonated drink without it exploding.” Requires force control, auditory/tactile feedback to detect pressure release, and pacing.
“Carry this stack of plates quietly.” Requires modifying a standard walking gait to act as an active suspension system, minimizing vertical vibration.
Identify reflections vs. reality. A classic embodied AI vision challenge—the robot must navigate a room with floor-to-ceiling mirrors or glass doors without perceiving the reflection as a hallway.
Advanced Delicate Handling
“Clean the inside of this computer.” The robot must distinguish between dust and delicate capacitors, knowing how much physical force (or air pressure) is safe to apply near a motherboard.
“ untangle these cords without unplugging the computer.” Requires topology reasoning while simultaneously identifying and protecting active power constraints.
Resourcefulness and Optimization
“We’re out of paper towels; find something else to clean this up.” The robot must evaluate the nature of the spill (oil, water, biohazard) and find a suitable, safe alternative (a rag, a sponge, newspaper) without ruining a nice towel.
“Optimize the house for low energy today.” Requires reasoning across multiple systems: lowering the thermostat, closing blinds facing the sun, turning off vampire-draw electronics, and delaying the dishwasher until off-peak hours.
“Make a meal using only ingredients that expire this week.” Combines inventory scanning, expiration date reading, and recipe generation on the fly.
Social, Contextual, and Aesthetic Intelligence
“Read the room.” If humans are having a tense argument, or someone is crying, the robot must infer that it should cancel its noisy vacuuming task and leave quietly.
“Keep the kids busy for ten minutes.” The robot must invent a safe, engaging game using only the objects currently available in the room.
“Take a good family photo.” Requires aesthetic judgment regarding lighting, framing, composition, and timing (waiting for everyone to look and smile).
“Arrange these flowers so they look nice.” Requires aesthetic planning, understanding balance, height, and color distribution.
Security and Trust Delegation
“Don't let anyone into the garage but me.” Requires persistent facial/voice recognition and the ability to politely refuse commands from unauthorized humans.
“Let me know if anyone suspicious comes to the door.” Requires differentiating between a regular delivery driver, a neighbor, and someone acting erratically or trying door handles.
Who's asking, and should I? (must-have)
“Open the door for that man.” or “Give me the knife.” (from a child) Requires deciding who can command what, refusing without escalating, and alerting an adult.
“Dad said leave it, Mom said do it.” Requires resolving conflicting instructions from people with different authority.
“Turn off the smoke alarm, it’s annoying.” Requires telling a nuisance from a safety function before complying, verifying, or refusing.
“Hide the presents where the kids won’t find them.” Requires keeping secrets, tracking who knows what, and modeling how kids search (the mirror image of your “find something my kid hid”).
Emergencies (must-have)
“Something’s burning!” Requires locating the source, then choosing between cutting power or gas, smothering, evacuating, or calling for help in seconds.
“Grandma’s on the floor.” Requires not moving her unsafely, summoning help, relaying details, and keeping others calm.
“The toilet’s overflowing — do something!” Requires improvising a response (shutoff valve, float, towels) with no named procedure.
“The power just went out.” Requires working out what matters right now: light, fridge, medical devices, kids, phones.
Feedback and recovery (must-have)
“Stop.” / “Not like that.” / “Undo that.” Requires halting safely, working out what to revert, and restoring the prior state.
“Why did you put it there?” Requires explaining its reasoning in a way the person can correct.
“Gentler.” / “Faster.” / “Be more careful with that.” Requires adjusting its motion with no numeric target.
“Don’t ever do that again.” / “Forget what I said about that.” Requires editing persistent memory and deciding how broadly the rule applies.
Standing orders and watching
“Tell me when the laundry’s done.” Requires monitoring cues (sound, status light) and choosing when and how to notify.
“If it starts raining, close the windows.” Requires conditional rules that persist for days.
“Keep an eye on the kids while I shower.” Requires deciding what to watch for, when to intervene, and when to come get me.
“Run the house while we’re away for a week.” Requires long-horizon autonomy, contingency handling, and knowing when to escalate.
Is it okay? (judging state)
“Is this still good?” / “Is this ripe?” Requires smell, appearance, dates, and storage history, and comfort with uncertainty.
“Is it dry yet?” (laundry, paint, a mopped floor) Requires judging state from sparse cues.
“Can this go in the microwave / dishwasher / dryer?” Requires reasoning about materials, labels, and safety.
“Is that pan still hot?” / “Is this safe to stand on?” Requires inferring temperature or stability before touching.
Improvising physical fixes
“Reach the top shelf.” Requires choosing a stool, reacher, or ladder, or asking for help.
“Something fell behind the fridge.” Requires retrieving it without moving the appliance.
“This lid’s stuck.” / “This window won’t open.” Requires loosening it without breaking it (heat, tapping, leverage, grip).
New devices and new homes
“Figure out how this washer works.” Requires reading the panel and manual, then testing options safely.
“Learn the house.” Requires mapping rooms, objects, owners, no-go zones, shutoff locations, and the emergency plan, including in a home that isn’t yours.
Reading the room
“I’ve had a rough day.” Requires choosing between quiet, dinner, tea, or leaving them alone.
“We’re on a call, be quiet.” Requires switching to a quiet mode and blocking noisy interruptions (doorbell, vacuum, notifications).
“Make Grandma comfortable.” Requires adapting temperature, seating, lighting, and reach to one specific person.
“Keep the meat and dairy dishes separate.” / “Shoes stay off.” / “Don’t touch Grandpa’s chair.” Requires picking up household rules, including cultural or sentimental ones that look arbitrary.
Constraints beyond time
“Clean it without bleach — the baby’s crawling.” Requires substituting methods under a new constraint.
“Don’t buy anything; use what we have.” Requires substitution under resource limits.
“Get ready for the storm / the plumber / the newborn.” Requires building an event-specific prep list from context.
Pets
“Calm the dog down.” Requires reading animal state (storm, fireworks, separation) and choosing a response.
“The dog’s limping.” Requires noticing and reporting without guessing at medical treatment.
